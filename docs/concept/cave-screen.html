Inside the cave: a separate screen

The sea encounters stay small, a card over the world. A cave is different: the ship stays at the mouth and the whole screen becomes the inside. Everything is drawn at 320x180 and scaled up, with the same dithering and palette as the game. Four ways to do it, all using the existing scenes and choices. Any cave can use any layout.

A · The passage. A first-person look down the cave: rock rings close in on a lit chamber, the object of the scene sits in the light (here a chest on a ledge), and water laps at the bottom. A tide gauge on the right rises with every room, so the pressure is visible. Choices sit in the same blue-and-gold box the villagers use. Best for tense caves.

The Hermit's Larder

B · The lantern circle. The screen is black except for a circle of lamplight. Whatever is in the circle is the scene: shelves, jars, a hermit by the kettle. The circle shifts and the light flickers between rooms. Dialogue uses the greeting-box layout with a portrait, so cave people talk like villagers. Best for comic and quiet caves, and it is the cheapest to build.

C · The cutaway. A cross-section of the rock, like a ship's plan: your ship at the surface, a passage down, three chambers lit one after another. The room you are in glows, the rooms behind you stay dim, and the next one is dark. You always see how deep you are and that going back means a longer climb. Best for the "one-time" feeling: the map of the cave is the whole quest.

D · The gamebook page. A framed picture on the left and a parchment page on the right with a paragraph number, like a roleplaying gamebook ("turn to § 12"). The d20 result stays visible at the bottom of the page after each roll. It is the most "dungeon master" option and the most readable for longer scenes, but it feels the furthest from the sailing.
